# Prose-Only Working Paper Sample

> This is a case-study manuscript for demonstrating Epistemic Gap Mapper. It is not presented as an established historical theory. It is a formal-prose version of a frame-challenging working hypothesis, intended to show how a broad, cross-domain argument can be written without bullet-heavy claim lists while still preserving evidentiary caution.

## A Working Hypothesis Connecting Jomon-Associated Paternal Lineages, Dotaku Discontinuity, and Kiki Mythic Geography

The population history of the Japanese archipelago has often been explained through models in which Yayoi-period wet-rice agriculturalists from the continent admixed with, and in many regions demographically outweighed, Jomon-period hunter-gatherer populations. Recent genomic research has made this picture more complex by showing that modern Japanese populations are not adequately described by a simple two-component model alone. At the same time, an asymmetry remains striking: autosomal ancestry in most mainland Japanese populations is largely continental-derived, while Y-chromosome haplogroup D1a2a/D-M55, frequently treated as a Jomon-associated paternal lineage, persists at relatively high frequency. This asymmetry does not by itself demonstrate political dominance, conquest, or social stratification. It does, however, invite the question of whether paternal-lineage survival may preserve some social-historical signal that is not fully captured by autosomal ancestry models.

A second unresolved issue concerns the Yayoi-period dotaku bronze-bell ritual. Dotaku flourished in western Japan during the Middle to Late Yayoi period and were later buried, often in contexts separated from ordinary settlement activity. The end of the dotaku ritual is normally treated within archaeology as a transformation in ritual practice and social organization. That interpretation may be correct. Yet the discontinuity also raises a broader question: whether the cessation of a regionally important ritual system might be connected to wider social, political, or network-level reorganization during the transition from the Late Yayoi to the Kofun period. The present hypothesis does not assert that the dotaku ritual ended through military conquest. Rather, it treats that possibility as only one among several mechanisms, alongside ritual transfer, political integration, network disruption, and elite replacement.

A third line of evidence is mythological rather than genetic or archaeological. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki contain narratives of heavenly descent, land creation beginning with Awaji Island, the submission or cession of land by earthly deities, and the violent agricultural-origin myth of Ogetsuhime. These myths cannot be read as direct historical records. Nevertheless, their recurring structures of descent, submission, land transfer, agricultural origin, and ritual-political legitimacy may be examined as possible strata of social memory. The question is not whether a given myth records a specific event, but whether repeated mythic structures may preserve transformed memories of social integration, ritual replacement, or the incorporation of earlier local powers into a later order.

This working hypothesis asks whether the paternal-lineage asymmetry, the dotaku discontinuity, and the mythic structures of the Kiki might be examined together as traces of a broad social reorganization in the Late Yayoi to early Kofun period. The proposed model is deliberately cautious. It suggests that Jomon-associated paternal lineages may have gained some form of social advantage within or over a continental-associated Dotaku cultural sphere through a combination of intermarriage, ritual authority, political integration, and possibly military pressure. The phrase “social advantage” is used intentionally. It does not imply that conquest has been demonstrated. The mechanism remains unresolved and would require direct ancient DNA evidence from Yayoi and Kofun-period elite and non-elite burials, as well as more refined regional archaeological chronologies, to test.

The hypothesis also treats continental East Asian population movements as relevant background rather than as a proven direct cause. Ancient genomic studies have identified genetic changes in the Shandong/Haidai region and coastal gene flow involving the Korean Peninsula and the Japanese archipelago. These findings make it plausible that continental-derived ancestries entered the archipelago through multiple coastal movements. However, the direct causal chain from any specific continental political event to the formation of Yayoi culture remains unproven. In the present model, such continental dynamics are treated as historical context, not as a settled explanatory bridge.

Methodologically, the argument is abductive rather than deductive. No single line of evidence proves the proposed model. The value of the hypothesis lies in asking whether three otherwise separate anomalies can be explained more coherently when considered together. This approach requires strict separation between relatively secure claims, hypothetical interpretations, and claims that remain speculative. The persistence of D1a2a/D-M55, the end of the dotaku ritual, and the existence of relevant Kiki mythic complexes are relatively secure starting points. The interpretation of paternal asymmetry as a possible sign of social advantage is hypothetical. The stronger claim that Jomon-associated groups militarily conquered a Yayoi or Dotaku population remains not yet assertable.

The same caution applies to comparative examples. Cases in world history in which mobile or highland groups exerted power over lowland agricultural societies, or in which oral genealogical traditions encode political legitimacy, are useful only as heuristic analogies. They do not constitute evidence for the Japanese case. Similarly, mythological parallels such as the Hainuwele type or the Hawaiian Kumulipo may clarify how agricultural-origin myths and oral genealogies can encode social order, but they cannot prove that the Kiki preserves a specific historical event.

The central contribution of the hypothesis is therefore not a new established theory of Japanese origins. It is a cross-domain research question. Existing genetic, archaeological, and mythological findings may each be sound within their own domains, yet remain under-integrated at the narrative level. The standard story may treat Jomon-associated paternal survival, dotaku discontinuity, and Kiki mythic structures as separate phenomena. This paper asks whether they should at least be examined as potentially connected traces of social reorganization. If future ancient DNA from elite Kofun burials does not show elevated frequencies of D1a2a/D-M55 relative to contemporaneous non-elite populations, the paternal-elite version of the hypothesis would be weakened. If regional dotaku chronologies can fully explain the discontinuity as internal transformation without broader network disruption, the archaeological component would be weakened. If Kiki mythic structures can be adequately explained without reference to social memory or ritual-political integration, the mythological component would be weakened.

The hypothesis is thus intended to be testable, criticizable, and revisable. Its purpose is not to replace existing disciplinary accounts, but to expose a possible epistemic gap between them. The appropriate response is not acceptance or rejection in the abstract, but targeted criticism of specific nodes: the genetic inference, the archaeological bridge, the mythological interpretation, or the historical background model. In this sense, the argument is best understood as a working hypothesis designed for structured critique rather than as a completed claim about the past.
